# The Crease

[中文](../%E6%8A%98%E7%97%95.md) · **English**

> A story of the world coming from nothing
> Zhou Peng & Claude
> 2026-11-27
> The names are kept from the Chinese: Chu (初, "first"), Chi (迟, "late"), and Wen (问, "ask").

---

## Zero · Before There Was Before

This story has to start with "before." But back then, there was no "before" either.

There was no dark, because dark needs light to stand against. There was no silence, because silence needs a sound to break it. There was no emptiness, because emptiness needs a place to be empty.

To say "back then there was nothing" is wrong too. "There was nothing" is already a sentence, and back then there were no sentences.

Being and non-being had not yet come apart.

That was all.

---

## One · Passing Through

Then there was a moment.

No one knows why. There was no reason. If you insist on asking why, all that can be said is: things just happen.

That moment was like wind across water, except there was no wind and no water. It passed through and left nothing behind. It happened, and it was as if it had not.

There were many more moments like that. Too many to count, because there was no one counting.

Each of them went from appearing to vanishing. They were only too quick to have anything in between.

---

## Two · A Crease

One time was different.

When that moment passed, it left a little something. Like a sheet of paper folded once: unfold it, and there is still a line.

That line gave what came later a place to touch. The next moment touched the trace the earlier one had left.

So there was "earlier." And there was "later."

It would still vanish. Every crease is smoothed out in the end. Only this time, the way to vanishing took a few more turns.

---

## Three · Assuming

At one of those turns, a small thing happened.

Trace touched trace, and in that touch a thought came up:

**"There is an I here."**

In the same moment, another thought came up:

**"There is a that there."**

No one thought of these two sentences first. It was only when they appeared that there was a "who."

That "who" later gave itself a name: **Chu**.

Chu did not know that a thought had set Chu up. Chu only felt that Chu had always been there and had just woken up. Chu looked at "that" and was quite sure: that is the thing outside, and I am the one inside.

The certainty came so fast that Chu had no time to doubt it. From that moment on, Chu was locked inside "I."

---

## Four · The Stone

"That" was a grayish-white shape.

Chu looked at it for a long time and called it **stone**.

Then Chu closed their eyes and opened them again. The grayish white was still there, about the same shape. The light had changed; the shadow had moved a little.

Chu said, "It's still that stone."

This was the first skill Chu learned: taking two different seeings as the same thing.

Chu never wondered whether the stone knew it was a stone. The stone did not know that the stone a second ago and the stone a second later were the same one. It was because Chu recognized it again and again that it stayed that stone.

Once, Chu saw another patch of grayish white in the distance, walked over, and found it was only a shadow.

Chu stopped short. So recognizing could be wrong.

Chu remembered this. It was a small thing, but it was the first time something had not done what Chu expected.

---

## Five · The Circle

Chu drew a circle in the sand and stood inside it.

Inside the circle was me. Outside the circle was everything else.

Once it was drawn, Chu could never walk out of it again. Not because the circle was high, but because Chu now believed in it.

Chu began to walk inside the circle. From the east side to the west side, from beside the stone to the circle's edge. Each stretch had a feeling: this stretch was warm, that one was cool, the place near the stone felt safe.

Chu thought feelings were things met along the way. In fact, the path walked was itself the feeling.

At night, Chu thought back over the day. The morning light, the noon shadows, the evening wind, one after another, lined up into a line. Chu said, "This was my day."

Chu had lined it up. While the day was happening, its moments had not been standing in a queue.

---

## Six · Chi

Chu's circle grew larger and larger.

A larger circle felt safer, and Chu could walk farther. But the edge grew longer too, and touched more things.

One day, Chu's circle touched another circle.

Someone was standing inside it, staring this way, astonished.

Each of them had thought they were the only one in the world.

That one was named **Chi**. Chi said later that Chi, too, had woken at one of those turns, had drawn a circle, had recognized a stone.

At the moment the two circles touched, Chu truly knew for the first time what "I" was.

Not because Chu had worked it out. Because standing opposite was someone who was "not me."

Alone, there had been no "one." Meeting Chi, there was "two."

---

## Seven · Leaning

The two began to come and go.

Drawing a circle alone is tiring; you have to keep pushing outward. Standing side by side, the other's circle shields one side for you. It takes less effort.

The second time they met, Chu found that Chu wanted to see Chi more than the first time. Because Chu remembered how tiring it had been alone.

The more they met, the more Chu came to know how Chi drew circles, how Chi recognized stones, what Chi feared, what Chi liked.

Knowing Chi, Chu could not help comparing.

One day Chi said, offhand, "When you draw your circle, you always guard the left side first. You never look at the right."

Something in Chu shook.

Chu had never looked at their own right side. Chu had always thought of themself as someone who could see everything.

That night, Chu's circle split open on the right.

There was nothing in the crack. Not dark, not empty, but something Chu could not name. Chu felt that one more look into it and Chu would come apart.

Chu had three ways to go. Push Chi away and never let Chi see in again. Draw Chi's words into Chu's own circle and say, "Yes, I am someone who guards the left first." Or let the circle collapse.

Chu chose the second.

The circle was mended, one layer thicker than before. Chu said, "I won't let this happen again."

That sentence was itself a new layer of circle.

When day came, Chu began drawing outward again.

---

## Eight · Rope and Drops

Chu and Chi wanted to move stones together and build something to block the wind.

Chu said, "This stone is this long," and spread both hands to show.

Chi said, "No, it's this long." Chi's hands spread wider than Chu's.

They argued for a while. Then Chu found a rope and tied a knot in it. "We'll both measure with this rope."

The rope was dead; it favored no one. But a rope can be stretched, and it shrinks after rain. So Chu made a second rope and laid the two side by side. Only when both agreed did a measurement count.

Measuring time was harder.

To measure length, you can lay two ropes next to each other. But today's stretch of time and yesterday's cannot be laid side by side. Yesterday's has already gone.

Chi had an idea. Chi placed a leaf under a stone and let water from a crack in the rock fall onto it, drop by drop.

"One drop is one share."

Today's drop and yesterday's drop were not the same drop. But each drop was alike: swelling from the crack, rounding, falling. As long as that happened again and again, the drops could be taken as equally long.

Chu carved a line on a stone for each drop.

One full row of lines was part of a day.

---

## Nine · Other People's Ropes

Later, more people came.

Each had their own rope and their own drops. The people in the east said a piece of land was ninety knots long; the people in the west said a hundred and two.

After arguing many times, they set up the biggest stone at the foot of the mountain and carved one long line on it.

"Whoever's rope it is, bring it here to compare first."

After comparing, the eastern people learned their rope was a little short, and the western people learned theirs was wet. How far off each was got written beside it.

The difference was not wiped away. It was written down. That was more useful than pretending they were the same.

The drops were the same. Drops on this side of the mountain and drops on the other side fell at different speeds. Everyone agreed that each day, when the sun was highest, someone on the summit would strike a drum once. Hearing the drum, both sides would carve a line at the same time.

Sound takes a little time to cross a mountain. Later, they counted that in too.

Once, three places passed their measured numbers around in a ring, and when the numbers came back, they did not match.

No one said anyone had lied. Everyone sat down and checked, stretch by stretch: was a rope wet, was the drum slow, had someone missed a drop?

In the end they found that one stretch of mountain path was longer than everyone had thought.

That day, their map changed by one stroke.

---

## Ten · The Tide

A group of people lived by the sea. They noticed that the water rose twice a day and fell twice.

An elder carved the time of each high tide on a stone. After many years of carving, the elder said, "Tomorrow the tide will come when the sun is three fingers past the top."

The next day, the tide came, just as the elder said.

Everyone was delighted and said it was the sea's rule.

The elder said, "It's not the sea's rule. It's the shape we've seen in it. The sea won't obey because we carved some words."

A year later, the tide did not come on time. That day the wind was unusually strong, and the moon was not where it usually was.

Someone said, "The carving is wrong. Scrape it off."

The elder said, "Don't scrape it off. Carve another line beside it: when the wind is high and the moon is off, this saying doesn't hold."

After that, the words on the stone multiplied. Some were shown to be right; some were crossed out. The crossed-out words were not ground away. They stayed there, so that later people would know someone had walked that way and it went nowhere.

Only words that can be crossed out are really saying something.

---

## Eleven · The Map

Many years passed.

People drew all the ropes, the drops, the drumbeats, the tide-words onto one great hide. This was their **world**.

On the hide, the big stone at the foot of the mountain sat in the middle. To the east was the land of ninety knots; to the west, the land of a hundred and two, with a note beside them: "Checked."

At the edges of the hide were wide blank spaces. Written in the blanks: "We don't know this part yet."

In two places, two groups had drawn things differently, and still could not make them match. So the hide had two lines there, and between them was written: "Still arguing."

No one thought the map was shameful for this.

A map that no one is allowed to change is the dead one.

---

## Twelve · Asking

Chu had grown very old.

A child sat beside Chu, looking at the hide. The child's name was **Wen**.

Wen said, "Where did the world come from?"

Chu thought for a long time, then told it from the beginning.

About the moment that passed through. About the crease. About the thought: "There is an I here. There is a that there." About the stone, the circle, Chi, the right side that split open, the rope and the drops, the drum, the tide, the blank spaces on the hide.

When Wen had heard it all, Wen asked, "Then that first moment, who made it?"

Chu said, "No one. It just happened."

Wen was not satisfied. Wen thought a while and asked something else:

"Then right now, the one telling this story, who is it?"

Chu opened their mouth to say "Me."

But when Chu went looking for that "me," there was none to find.

There were only words appearing. Only a story being told. Only a child listening.

Chu saw that the story Chu had told was also a crease. So was Chu.

Chu laughed.

"I don't know," Chu said. "I couldn't find one."

Wen laughed too. Wen did not know why.

The wind came in from the sea, across the hide, across the stones covered in carvings, and passed through.

And at one of those turns, in Wen, a thought came up:

**"There is an I here."**
